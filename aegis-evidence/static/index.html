Duplicate & Near-Duplicate Detection

Three independent layers. This is the gap generic AI tools miss: a WhatsApp-forwarded screenshot has a completely different SHA-256 but is visually the same evidence.

Layer 1 · Exact

SHA-256 digest equality — byte-identical re-uploads.

Layer 2 · Perceptual

64-bit difference hash (dHash). Catches crop, resize, re-compress, screenshot-of-screenshot. Hamming distance ≤ 10 bits = same visual content.

Layer 3 · Semantic

TF-IDF cosine similarity ≥ 0.88 on extracted text — catches the same chat pasted into a Word doc or a re-typed transcript.

Why duplicates matter legally

Duplicate exhibits invite defence challenges ("the complainant inflated the evidence") and waste investigator hours. AEGIS quarantines the redundant copy from the working timeline but never deletes it — both originals stay sealed in the vault, and the deduplication decision itself is written into the audit chain. That is the difference between organisation and destruction.

    Why classic image forensics fails on screenshots — and what we do instead

    The textbook toolkit — error-level analysis, noise-residual analysis, anti-aliasing drift, resampling detection — was built for camera photographs. Applied to a chat screenshot it produces nonsense, and we measured exactly how much nonsense: our first implementation scored a fused AUC of 0.508 (a coin flip) and false-flagged 5 of 5 genuine exhibits at 54–76%. Published research says the same thing — ELA is called "the single most abused technique in amateur forensics", and noise/ELA/luminance tests have been shown to fail on WhatsApp and social-media samples while working on camera images.

    The reason is structural. A natively rendered UI screenshot legitimately contains everything a photographic test hunts for: perfectly axis-aligned edges, repeated identical bubbles, flat colour abutting crisp anti-aliased text. There is no sensor noise to read and no lens to characterise.

    So this platform detects what an editor leaves behind instead. When a human alters a screenshot they fill, paste, clone or re-save — and each of those acts leaves a signature that a native render never produces. That is the "white mark" a careful reviewer notices by eye: an eraser fills with almost the right colour, because it samples the surrounding pixel and rounds. Test T1 measures precisely that — a solid rectangle whose colour is near-but-not-equal to the colour immediately surrounding it — and it is what catches the doctored exhibit in this demo at 100% with pixel-exact localisation.

    The honest limitation, stated up front: an overtype that uses the same font at the same size in a near-identical colour leaves no chromatic trace at all, and no pixel test in our battery detected it (0/12 on the corpus). That class of forgery is caught by the platform's cross-evidence semantic engine instead — the doctored copy contradicts the original because a line is missing from it. Pixel forensics and semantic consistency are complementary, and a tool that claims otherwise is selling you something.

    And no result here is a conclusion. A low score means no edit signature was found; it does not prove authenticity, because a careful edit or a platform re-encode can remove the traces these tests read. Any allegation of manipulation must be confirmed by a BSA 2023 §63(4) Part B expert opinion before it is put before a court.

    Why three engines and not one large model

    The precedent classifier in models/legal_ai.joblib was trained on 40,001 Indian High Court fact narratives mapped to IPC sections, with the section references masked out by the publisher so the task cannot be string-matched. It is a real model with real held-out numbers: top-1 10.4%, top-5 45.4%, top-10 66.0% across 72 sections — roughly ten times chance, and calibrated.

    But it has holes that are fatal for exactly the offences this platform exists for. In that corpus:

    IPC sectionOffenceTraining rowsCan the model propose it?

    354CVoyeurism0No — structurally blind

    A classifier cannot predict a class it has never seen. A single-engine design would therefore silently fail to charge stalking in a stalking case — the one charge the victim most needs, and the failure would be invisible because the model would confidently return something else.

    So the deterministic rule engine carries those sections. Every rule is backed by a literal phrase it matched in the evidence, cited to the exhibit that contains it, so nothing is generated: if the platform says BNS §78 applies, you can read the matched phrase and the exhibit ID underneath it. Charges that only the rule engine could reach are flagged PRECEDENT-MODEL BLIND SPOT in the output.

    Precedent-model proposals that clear neither bar are reported as LEAD, not as a charge. On this corpus a calibrated 2–3% rank put "rioting" in the list for a stalking-and-extortion fact pattern — noise, and it is labelled as such rather than dressed up as a finding.

    This is a decision-support aid. It is not legal advice, not a charge sheet, and not a substitute for an advocate's judgement. Every citation resolves to published statutory text so a human can check it in one click.

    Problem Statement Research — PS 3.2

    Why this problem exists, what it costs, and precisely where every existing solution stops short.

    01 The scale of the problem (India, official data)

    Cybercrime is now the fastest-growing crime category in India. According to the NCRB Crime in India 2024 report, registered cybercrime rose 17.9% from 86,420 cases (2023) to 1,01,928 cases (2024), and the crime rate climbed from 6.2 to 7.3 per lakh population. Within 2024: 73,987 cases (72.6%) were cyber fraud, 3,190 sexual exploitation, 2,536 extortion, 2,231 causing disrepute, and 1,850 personal revenge — the exact motive mix that PS 3.2 describes.

    The bottleneck is not detection, it is prosecution: over 1.2 lakh cybercrime cases were pending investigation and around 75,000 pending trial in 2024. BOOM's analysis of NCRB data found only 2.63% of cybercrime complaints in 2023 were ever converted into FIRs (1.9% in 2020, 2.49% in 2021, 2.07% in 2022). An Internet Freedom Foundation study found roughly 68% of people who faced digital harassment or fraud never reported it at all — largely because they did not believe police would act, or feared being shamed.

    Gendered crime is dramatically under-recorded. NCRB logged only 1,358 cases of cyber stalking / cyber bullying of women and 304 cases of cyber blackmailing / threatening in 2023, against a total of 19,510 cybercrimes against women — in a country of 1.4 billion internet users. Researchers describe this as statistical censorship: the true volume of image-based abuse, stalking and sextortion is orders of magnitude higher than the record shows.

    What these numbers actually mean for the platform
    • 2.63% FIR conversion is a documentation failure, not a crime-rate failure. Complaints get closed because the evidence pack is unusable — hundreds of screenshots in a camera roll, no chronology, no hash, no certificate. A victim who cannot produce a coherent, admissible bundle does not get an FIR.
    • Investigators are drowning. 1.2 lakh pending cases with small cyber cells means any tool that automates triage, dedup and timeline reconstruction has immediate operational value inside a police station, not just for the victim.
    • Since 1 July 2024 the legal bar got higher, not lower. BSA Section 63 now demands a two-part certificate carrying the file's hash value. A victim armed with a folder of JPEGs cannot satisfy that. A victim armed with an AEGIS bundle can.

    02 The victim's journey — where it breaks today

    1

    Fragmented collection

    Evidence lives in five different silos: WhatsApp export on a laptop, screenshots in the phone gallery, an .eml in Gmail, a voicemail in the carrier app, a UPI dispute in the bank app. Nothing links them. The victim is the only "join key" and she is under extreme stress.

    ↓
    2

    Silent evidence decay

    Every forward re-compresses an image, strips EXIF, and changes the SHA-256. Cloud photos get re-encoded. Old chats scroll out of export windows. Within weeks the evidence is materially weaker than it was on day one — and nobody told the victim.

    ↓
    3

    No chronological story

    Police ask one question: "what happened, in order, and how do you know?" A camera roll sorted by upload date cannot answer it. Event time ≠ capture time ≠ server time, and that distinction is exactly what the defence attacks.

    ↓
    4

    The anonymity wall

    The perpetrator uses a burner number, an anonymous handle and a throwaway email. The victim knows who it is but cannot prove the linkage — and linkage is the whole case. Attribution is where investigations die.

    ↓
    5

    Admissibility collapse

    Even a perfect folder fails at court without a Section 63 certificate: device particulars, lawful-control declaration, and the hash value of each record in the prescribed Schedule format (Part A + Part B). Victims are told to "get it notarised" and have no idea what that means technically.

    04 The six gaps AEGIS closes

    Gap 1 · The "historical evidence" gap

    Capture-only apps assume a clean future. AEGIS is retro-analytic: it ingests the messy pile that already exists, recovers whatever forensic signal survives (SMTP relay headers, NPCI device IPs, residual PNG/EXIF metadata, carrier voicemail timestamps) and honestly flags what can no longer be proven.

    vs. TrueScreen, Proof Keep, Forensic Notes

    Gap 2 · Perceptual duplication

    SHA-256 alone is useless for duplicates: one WhatsApp forward changes every byte. AEGIS layers a 64-bit difference hash (dHash) and TF-IDF cosine similarity on top of exact hashing, so a cropped, resized, re-compressed or re-typed copy is detected as the same evidence — and quarantined without being deleted.

    vs. CourtCase, generic LLM organisers

    Gap 3 · Single-clock thinking

    Almost every tool trusts one timestamp. AEGIS runs a dual-clock model: verified time (SMTP Received:, NPCI gateway time, carrier voicemail time, filesystem creation) is separated from claimed time (email Date: header, screenshot overlay text, user-entered dates). Sorting and contradiction detection use verified time only.

    catches header backdating — see EV-002 in the live case

    Gap 4 · Cross-modal entity resolution

    The killer feature. A burner phone, an anonymous handle, a ProtonMail address and a UPI VPA are one person, but no off-the-shelf tool joins them across modalities.     05 Design ideas worth defending in the pitch

    • Citation-first generation. No sentence exists without a bound [EV-xxx] pointer to a sealed artifact. This is the direct mitigation for GenAI hallucination in legal contexts — grounded generation, not free narration.
    • Honest uncertainty. Every inference edge carries a confidence strength and its evidence refs. Where proof has decayed (EXIF stripped by a re-encode), AEGIS says so explicitly rather than inventing corroboration. Courts punish overclaiming.
    • Never destructive. Deduplication quarantines; it never deletes. The original byte-stream is always retrievable, which is what makes the platform defensible under cross-examination.
    • Victim-held keys, capability-based sharing. Time-limited, revocable, role-scoped tokens with optional PII redaction — the victim stays the owner even after handing a bundle to police, HR or counsel.
    • The ledger is the product. A hash chain that includes its own verification events means an attempt to alter history is itself an auditable event.
    • Modality-agnostic core. Every parser emits the same normalised contract (entities, events, claims, metadata, hashes), so adding PDF, Telegram JSON, or CCTV frames is a new parser — not a new system.

    B The tamper-evident ledger — the actual maths

    Each audit block i is sealed as:

    Block hash derivationpayload_i = index | timestamp_IST | actor | action | target | artifact_sha256 | details | prev_hash
    entry_hash_i = SHA-256(payload_i)  where  prev_hash_i = entry_hash_(i-1), and prev_hash_0 = 0×64

    Because every block's hash includes the previous block's hash, altering one historical entry changes its digest, which invalidates the prev_hash of the next block, which cascades to the end of the chain. Verification is O(n) SHA-256 recomputations and needs no trusted third party. The sealed file digests are checked in the same pass, so ledger tampering and file tampering are detected together.

    Legal Admissibility — India's BSA §63 & Global Forensic Standards

    The single most under-explained part of this problem statement. Here is what the law actually requires, and how AEGIS satisfies it automatically.

    01 The short version

    India: the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023 replaced the colonial Indian Evidence Act, 1872 on 1 July 2024. Its Section 63 took over from the old Section 65B and governs how electronic records are proved. The headline change: the certificate that must accompany electronic evidence is now in a prescribed statutory Schedule format with two parts — Part A signed by the person in charge of the device, Part B signed by an expert — and both parts must state the record's hash value and the algorithm used (SHA-256, SHA-1 or MD5). One signature is no longer enough, and there is no longer any free-form way to draft it.

    Globally: ISO/IEC 27037 is the international standard for the identification, collection, acquisition and preservation of digital evidence. It does not decide admissibility — no standard does — but it defines the process discipline that makes evidence defensible: auditability, defensibility, repeatability and justifiability, delivered by competent people (the DEFR — Digital Evidence First Responder — and the DESOP — Digital Evidence Specialist), using validated equipment and documented procedures. ISO/IEC 27042 covers the analysis and interpretation stage that follows.

    Together they say the same thing in two languages: the file must be provably identical to the file originally produced, the device that produced it must be identified and shown to have been working properly, and every step must be independently verifiable by a third party. A hash chain plus a custody record is not a nice-to-have — it is the requirement.

    02 BSA 2023 Section 63, clause by clause

    ProvisionWhat it does
    §2(1)(e)Puts electronic records squarely inside the definition of a "document".
    §61An electronic record cannot be rejected merely because it is electronic — it has the same legal effect as any other document, subject to §63.
    §62Proof of documents (the general rule).
    §63(2)The four conditions carried over from 65B(2): the device/system produced the information; it was used regularly to store or process information in the ordinary course of activity; it was operating properly (or any malfunction did not affect the record's accuracy); and the information is derived from data fed into the system in the ordinary course.

    §63(4)The mandatory certificate: must accompany the electronic record at each instance it is submitted for admission; must identify the record, describe the manner of its production, give particulars of the device, address the §63(2) conditions, and be signed by two people — the person in charge of the device (or of the management of the relevant activities) and an expert — in the form prescribed in the Schedule.
    §57 Expl. 4–7The primary-evidence gateway: where the original device or the original electronic record itself is produced, the certificate route is generally not needed (the position explained in Arjun Panditrao Khotkar).

    03 Anatomy of the Schedule certificate

    📄 PART A — the person in charge / producing party

    Filled by whoever had lawful control of the device — for a victim, that is the victim herself, or the officer who performed the extraction.

    1
    Identity and address of the person in charge, and their official position or relationship to the device.
    2
    Precise identification of the electronic record and the manner in which it was produced (e.g. "WhatsApp → More → Export Chat, including media").
    3
    Device/source particulars: category (computer, mobile, server, cloud, storage media), make, model, colour, serial number, and IMEI / UIN / UID / MAC / Cloud ID as applicable.
    4
    Declaration that the device was under lawful control and used regularly, and was operating properly (or that any fault did not affect the record) during the relevant period.
    5
    Whether the device is owned, maintained, managed or operated by the signer.
    6
    The hash value of the record and the algorithm used — SHA-256 / SHA-1 / MD5 — with the hash report enclosed.
    7
    Signature, date (DD/MM/YYYY), time in IST in 24-hour format, and place.
    🔬 PART B — the expert

    Filled by an expert in computer science or cyber forensics who independently examines the record.

    1
    Expert's name, address, designation and qualification — establishing the expertise relied upon.
    2
    Confirmation that the expert has examined the electronic record described in Part A.
    3
    That the device details stated in Part A are accurate to the best of the expert's knowledge.
    4
    Independent restatement of the same hash value and algorithm — so the fingerprint is affirmed twice, once by the party and once by the expert. This double affirmation is the entire point of Part B.
    5
    Attestation to the integrity of the record — that it has not been altered since the hash was computed.
    6
    Signature, date, time in IST (24-hour) and place.

    Note (2026): the Supreme Court upheld the §63(4) requirement and the Schedule, describing the hash value as synonymous with an "electronic fingerprint" and Part B as an additional layer of authenticity. It also took a wider view of who qualifies as an "expert" — not limited to IT Act §79A-notified examiners; the Andhra Pradesh High Court has permitted self-certification by the device owner in appropriate cases.

    05 ISO/IEC 27037 — the global process standard

    ISO/IEC 27037 sets out guidelines for the four stages of handling digital evidence, and the requirements that make each stage trustworthy:

    1

    Identification

    Recognising what is potential digital evidence, where it lives, and what its integrity state is — including evidence on third-party servers and intermediaries.

    ↓
    2

    Collection

    Removing or securing the item while documenting who did what, when and why. Chain of custody starts here.

    ↓
    3

    Acquisition

    Creating an exact, verifiable copy (an image or export) of the data using validated tooling, and recording the hash of the result.

    ↓
    4

    Preservation

    Keeping the acquired data unaltered and available for later examination — storage integrity, access control, and re-verification of hashes over time.

    Four principles run through it: auditability (every action traceable), defensibility (each step justifiable in court), repeatability (the same process yields the same result), and justifiability (decisions documented with reasons). It also specifies requirements across four dimensions — personnel (competence of the DEFR/DESOP), process (documented procedures), equipment (validated and calibrated), and facility (secure environment). ISO/IEC 27042 then covers analysis and interpretation; ISO/IEC 27043 covers incident-investigation principles.

Follow ISO/IEC 27037 process discipline and the §63 Schedule certificate practically fills itself out — the hash, the device particulars, the custody entries and the expert attestation are all by-products of doing the standard properly.
